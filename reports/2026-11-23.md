### 1. AskWise: Execution-Guided Clarification of Intent and Data in Text-to-SQL

- arXiv ID: `2611.27474v1`
- Authors: Yuru Liang, Guangyinan Mei, Miles Fan
- Published: 2026-11-20
- Categories: `cs.DB`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27474v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.27474v1)

Two interpretations of a database question can agree on the observed records and diverge after a correction. A single record can therefore determine whether the user needs to clarify their intent. AskWise executes candidate queries across possible repairs. It asks about a choice only when two executions differ in that choice alone and give different answers. Once all surviving combinations give the same known answer, it returns that answer. For Boolean answers on a uniform binary cube, we derive an exact bound on the difference in EC2 gain between two questions from partial execution results. Across 120 sales tasks spanning three query types, the bound reduces mean executions per episode from 14.88 to 13.80 relative to a partition relaxation. Asking at the first sign that a choice matters uses 13.18 executions and 0.025 more questions per episode. On AmbiQT, a controlled value swap separates the reference queries in 424 of 1,206 eligible cases. On 155 sampled questions, pruning the same model candidates across both database states raises recall of reference answer signatures by 8.71 percentage points for Doubao Lite and 10.00 for GPT Sol.

### 2. Testability of Homomorphism Inadmissibility: Property Testing Meets Database Theory

- arXiv ID: `2611.26861v1`
- Authors: Hubie Chen, Yuichi Yoshida
- Published: 2026-11-19
- Categories: `cs.DB`, `cs.CC`, `cs.DS`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26861v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.26861v1)

In this paper, we utilize the perspective of property testing to consider the testability of relational database queries. A primary motivation is the desire to avoid reading an entire database to decide a property thereof. We focus on conjunctive queries, which are the most basic and heavily studied database queries. Each conjunctive query can be represented as a relational structure $\mathbf{A}$ such that deciding if the conjunctive query is satisfied by a relational structure $\mathbf{B}$ is equivalent to deciding if there exists a homomorphism from $\mathbf{A}$ to $\mathbf{B}$. We phrase our results in terms of homomorphisms.
  Precisely, we study, for each relational structure $\mathbf{A}$, the testability of homomorphism inadmissibility from $\mathbf{A}$. We consider algorithms that have oracle access to an input relational structure $\mathbf{B}$ and that distinguish, with high probability, the case where there is no homomorphism from $\mathbf{A}$ to $\mathbf{B}$, from the case where one needs to remove a constant fraction of tuples from $\mathbf{B}$ in order to suppress all such homomorphisms. We provide a complete characterization of the structures $\mathbf{A}$ from which one can test homomorphism inadmissibility with one-sided error by making a constant number of queries to $\mathbf{B}$. Our characterization shows that homomorphism inadmissibility from $\mathbf{A}$ is constant-query testable with one-sided error if and only if the core of $\mathbf{A}$ is $α$-acyclic. We also show that the injective version of the problem is constant-query testable with one-sided error if $\mathbf{A}$ is $α$-acyclic; this result generalizes existing results for testing subgraph-freeness in the general graph model.

### 3. MetaRigor: From Agentic Automation to Verifiable End-to-End Meta-Analysis Through Scientific Decision Boundaries

- arXiv ID: `2611.27713v1`
- Authors: Yifeng Xie, Chenke Li, Yangshuo Miao, Jingyuan Zheng, Mingxuan Liu, Feiwei Qin, YaQi Wang, Huiyu Zhou, Yifei Chen
- Published: 2026-11-20
- Categories: `cs.AI`, `cs.SE`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27713v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.27713v1)

Automated meta-analysis requires not only accurate numerical extraction but also the consistent preservation of result identity across stages, including the associated study, comparison, population, outcome, time point, and analysis conditions. We present MetaRigor, an LLM workflow framework for verifiable end-to-end evidence synthesis. MetaRigor organizes study results as Study x Target units and operationalizes scientific decision boundaries at the task level through Task Contracts, programmatic rules, and source binding. The model performs semantic interpretation that depends on study context, whereas programs execute formalizable rules, computations, validation, and source verification. We conducted controlled evaluations across five stages: evidence acquisition, data extraction, risk of bias assessment, certainty of evidence assessment, and manuscript generation. MetaRigor achieved an F1 score of 86.29% for complete result delivery and 80.20% agreement for domain-level certainty of evidence judgments. Generated manuscripts received scores of 86.00 under human evaluation, exceeding all three baselines. A seven-case downstream end-to-end evaluation further yielded an aggregate F1 score of 77.01%, exceeding the strongest baseline by 9.78 percentage points. Cross-system tracing of archived outputs from three systems further showed that local mismatches or omissions in result identity could alter statistical inputs and the objects subjected to risk of bias assessment, with these errors subsequently propagating into the final report. Ablation experiments showed that task constraints and programmatic rules played distinct roles across different stages. These findings indicate that, in LLM-driven evidence synthesis workflows, consistently maintaining explicit result identity, decision boundaries, and provenance relationships is critical to improving end-to-end verifiability.

### 4. Which Data-Center Load Models Can Be Trusted? An Evidence-Graded Review of Dynamic Modeling and Validation for Large Computational Loads

- arXiv ID: `2611.27169v1`
- Authors: Dileep Cheerla
- Published: 2026-11-20
- Categories: `eess.SY`, `cs.SE`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27169v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.27169v1)

Grid operators now require dynamic models of data centers and other large computational loads, and new ride-through rules assume that these models predict how facilities respond to disturbances. This review asks what evidence supports that assumption. We coded 160 documents, screened from 1,424 de-duplicated database and seed records plus 65 added documents, by the strongest reference object each presents, from grid-side records to simulation only. Across 19 field-evidence entries, 15 requirement documents from three continents and nine model classes, we find two comparisons of a standard library model with measured data-center events: with default parameters the model failed both, and refitting it to each event separately reduced the error but was never tested on an unseen event. Among the sources reviewed, we found no successful held-out validation of a frozen, facility-specific data-center model against an independent measured disturbance, and 25 of the 33 model-phenomenon combinations with any evidence involve no electrical measurement. The features that distinguish the new rules (memory of repeated dips, recovery within 0.5-3 s, proportional power reduction during the sag, overvoltage response) are those current phasor models cannot represent or have never been tested against. In the operator procedures examined, simulations and model-to-model benchmarks are accepted as evidence of model fidelity, unlike the measured validation required of generators, and proposed mitigations have mostly been evaluated on unvalidated facility models. We propose context-of-use gates that separate calibration from validation, release labels for model claims, a minimum validation protocol with acceptance criteria, and a research agenda.
