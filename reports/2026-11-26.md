### 1. Shape-Aware Three-Way Merge for RDF Knowledge Graphs: The schema defines the conflict: an implementation and evaluation

- arXiv ID: `2611.34870v1`
- Authors: Steve Brown
- Published: 2026-11-25
- Categories: `cs.DB`, `cs.AI`, `cs.SE`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34870v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.34870v1)

Merging RDF serializations creates ordering conflicts and misses semantic ones. We describe and evaluate a three-way merge in which the schema defines a conflict: set algebra combines canonical triples, and a slot is contended only where a SHACL shape bounds its cardinality and both sides' changes cannot stand within that bound. Unbounded predicates union; contended slots go to a person. The same shapes graph validates the result. We do not claim the first three-way merge for RDF. Our contribution is an implementation in a production multi-agent store and an evaluation against a reimplementation of Quit Store's Context Merge overlap rule, which it extends rather than replaces, and six other strategies. Across five synthetic seeds, the shape-aware operator raised 33 conflicts, all true positives as judged by the same shapes that define them, with no triples lost or fabricated and no SHACL violations admitted. The context-overlap baseline, counted per slot, detected the same 33 conflicts but asked 845 questions. A replay built from recorded production incidents covers 105 human alias repairs (93 after excluding 12 chained pairs) and 939 duplicate-value incidents. It reproduces a triple-level limit: different names for one entity remain invisible (0 of 93 alias repairs detected, matching 0 of 21 synthetic cases). In a study of evolving shape contracts, merging shape declarations first handles the tested cardinality changes, while datatype evolution exposes a detector blind spot caught by post-merge validation. An oracle that reads no shapes agrees with the shipped operator on the facts and history of all 23 reachable single-slot cases. Under wrong shapes, a missing bound loses data silently and a spurious one only adds questions. The supported claim is fewer human decisions with nothing silently lost, for cardinality conflicts in one deployment, not general merge correctness.

### 2. Single-Database Rewards Are Hackable: Counterfactual Execution for Trustworthy Text-to-SQL RL

- arXiv ID: `2611.34363v1`
- Authors: Shreshth Rai, Rishii Kumar Singh, Aksh Aggarwal
- Published: 2026-11-25
- Categories: `cs.LG`, `cs.CL`, `cs.DB`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34363v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.34363v1)

Reinforcement learning (RL) for text-to-SQL agents pays a query when its result matches the reference on one database. However, a wrong query can match by luck when the data lack the rows that expose its bug. We study a counterfactual-database (CF) reward that also runs the final query on a few hidden, code-generated copies of the database and pays only if all results match (strict form) or by the share matched (mean form). On 1,302 synthetic audit questions, the strict form lowers the share of buggy queries paid in full from 29.5% to 16.8% and of reward hacks from 54.1% to 1.9%, rejecting 0 of 2,847 correct queries; copies from a family-blind generator do about as well. In a controlled GRPO comparison on a 3.35B model with three seeds per arm, training on the mean form did not improve in-domain accuracy (8.4% vs 11.7%), because the two rewards disagreed on only 5-7% of rollouts; on Spider it gained 1.6 points. One seed reverses both signs, so single-seed reward comparisons mislead. Code: https://github.com/RishiiGamer2201/counterfactual-sql-rewards ; data, models, demo: https://huggingface.co/counterfactual-sql

### 3. Cache Replacement in Direct-Access Cloud OLAP-An Experimental Comparison and Practical Guidance

- arXiv ID: `2611.33977v1`
- Authors: Taejun Yoon, Taesung Lee, Joo Yeon Lee, Ki Hong Kim, Byungchul Tak, Wook-Shin Han
- Published: 2026-11-25
- Categories: `cs.DB`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33977v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.33977v1)

Direct-access cloud OLAP engines rely on local caches to hide remote object-store latency, yet the replacement design space remains poorly understood. Existing systems largely inherit recency- or Clock-oriented policies from DBMS buffer management, even though cloud misses combine a size-independent request overhead with a size-dependent transfer cost and cache objects vary widely in size. We present a broad experimental comparison of 22 cache-replacement algorithms and one semantic-caching baseline across seven analytical workloads and ten cache sizes. To compare them on a common basis, we formulate Unified Hit Ratio (UHR), a latency-weighted hit ratio that decomposes into a workload-specific weighted combination of object and byte hit ratios, and equivalently, the normalized fraction of aggregate remote-download latency saved by caching.
  Our results yield practitioner-oriented guidance. Classical size-aware policies developed outside the DBMS literature remain surprisingly strong, dominating object hit ratio on most workloads and staying near the UHR frontier. Byte efficiency depends primarily on aggressive filtering of zero-reuse objects, while under multi-tenant drift lightweight per-request adaptation is often more robust than retraining-heavy learned methods. We instantiate these lessons in LARA, a compact reference policy that validates the guidance through competitive UHR performance.

### 4. Data Agents Need a Map: Evolving, Demand-Driven Knowledge of Large Collections

- arXiv ID: `2611.33763v1`
- Authors: Christos Koutras, Yurong Liu, Laura Haas, Juliana Freire
- Published: 2026-11-25
- Categories: `cs.DB`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33763v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.33763v1)

Most data that could power rich analysis sits unintegrated and uncataloged in open data portals, data lakes, and collections of independently published tables. Data agents now answer analytical questions over such collections with a full workflow per question, including dataset retrieval, join discovery and evaluation, and tailored program synthesis, most of which are discarded when a new task arrives. The knowledge these workflows depend on, such as what each column means, which context a dataset leaves implicit, and how datasets combine, is therefore perpetually rederived and never persisted.
  We argue for persisting this knowledge in an evolving semantic atlas that users and agents can navigate and extend. The atlas describes every dataset offline, through topics, semantic types, and profiles, so that agents can find the datasets that a task requires. Relationships between datasets and integrated views, which require comparing datasets, are instead built on demand for the areas of analysis that users declare. Every item is stored with its provenance and the confidence of the methods that produced it, so that agents can decide how far to trust it or whether to abstain. As datasets change, inconsistencies are detected and corrected automatically, while human users settle what the data leaves inconclusive. We present this vision and its challenges, namely building the atlas over large, multi-domain collections, keeping it correct and up to date, serving it to users and agents, and evaluating whether it improves their answers. A proof of concept on DAB, a benchmark for data agents, shows that with only part of the atlas a coding agent can already read a fifth to a third fewer tokens while achieving the same accuracy as without it.
