### 1. Adaptive-Elbow Graph Traversal for Schema Retrieval in Text-to-SQL

- arXiv ID: `2611.20979v1`
- Authors: Fabio Picariello, Salvatore Cipolla, Susanna Bonura, Vito Morreale
- Published: 2026-11-16
- Categories: `cs.DB`, `cs.IR`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20979v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.20979v1)

LLM-based natural-language interfaces to relational databases are gated by a schema-retrieval step: the generator can only be as good as the slice of schema it is shown. On a database with hundreds of tables, that slice must be selected and the selection is two-sided---omit a needed table and the query becomes unrecoverable, include too many and the irrelevant ones act as noise that degrades the model's output. When an agentic retriever that lets the model inspect the schema can be too costly or too slow, the fallback is an embedder that ranks tables by cosine similarity and keeps a top-k. This fallback has two weaknesses: a fixed budget is suboptimal question by question, and embedding similarity overlooks foreign-key-linked tables that are necessary for joins. We present Adaptive-Elbow Graph Traversal (AEGT, ElbowHop for short), a property-graph table retriever that addresses both: an adaptive elbow cutoff replaces the fixed budget with a per-question one under a single aggressiveness parameter, and bidirectional foreign-key traversal deterministically recovers the bridge tables embedding similarity misses. We evaluate the retriever on 3 corpora of increasing candidate-pool size (BIRD Mini-Dev queried per database, 3--13 tables; its eleven schemas merged, 74; and a merged Spider-train graph, 546) and isolate the two mechanisms under a per-question budget-matched protocol. Across these settings the adaptive elbow emerges as the dominant mechanism---at matched recall it roughly doubles precision over a fixed budget on the larger pools, the margin widening as the pool grows---while the foreign-key traversal pays off on the join-critical bridge tables dense retrieval cannot reach, in the single-hop tight-budget case. We characterize the method as a low-cost, recall-robust first stage for schema retrieval on which a downstream selection or generation step can build.

### 2. Living Context Graphs: Continual Semantic Learning for Enterprise Data Reasoning

- arXiv ID: `2611.20976v1`
- Authors: Varun Agrawal, Ayush Srivastava, Eshan Gujar, Kaushal Kumar, Ayush Gupta
- Published: 2026-11-16
- Categories: `cs.CL`, `cs.AI`, `cs.DB`, `cs.ET`, `cs.IR`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20976v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.20976v1)

Enterprise AI can now perform analytics, reporting, and data-driven workflows, yet the data foundations it relies on remain hard to operationalize: business meaning, relationships, metric definitions, and reporting conventions are absent from database schemas and scattered across undocumented systems and tribal knowledge. We introduce the Living Context Graph (LCG), a continually learned context layer that makes existing enterprise data operational for AI without requiring a copy or transformation of the underlying warehouse. LCG bootstraps base semantics and relationships from the data itself, builds a concept graph that indexes business context as queries are served, writes durable semantic learnings from verified outcomes, and withdraws or re-earns context when later outcomes contradict prior routes. We evaluate LCG through text-to-SQL, a core data-reasoning task, on a transfer-aware split of three BIRD databases comprising 499 questions: 353 form the learning history and 146 are held out and never used to update the context layer. Bootstrapping alone improves overall accuracy by +4.6 percentage points. Learning only from the history raises accuracy on the untouched held-out transfer set by +22.6 points (95% CI [+14.4, +30.8]), exceeding five-shot retrieval from the same history by +12.3 points. Incremental learning reaches the one-shot endpoint within observed evaluation noise on every database. Ablating the graph changes held-out accuracy by at most one question per database but increases per-query cost 2.3-3.3x and median latency 1.8-3.4x: continually learned semantics drive accuracy, while the graph makes that context efficient to serve.

### 3. Tight Width Minimisation of Self-Join-Free Quantified Conjunctive Queries

- arXiv ID: `2611.20845v1`
- Authors: Hubie Chen, Fabio Mogavero, Sam M. Thompson
- Published: 2026-11-16
- Categories: `cs.DB`, `cs.LO`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20845v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.20845v1)

Query minimisation is a classical problem in database theory and the width of a formula, defined as the maximum number of free variables over all subformulae, is a central measure for query evaluation. We study width minimisation for a subclass of quantified conjunctive queries (QCQs), namely self-join-free QCQs. For this subclass, we show that rewriting optimality coincides with semantic optimality: given a query $φ$, the minimum-width query obtainable from $φ$ by applying natural and established rewriting rules has minimum width among all first-order formulae logically equivalent to $φ$. As a consequence, the width of a self-join-free QCQ can be optimised by a logical-equivalence-preserving rewriting algorithm.

### 4. QuarkDB: A Hybrid Database System for Quantum Data Management

- arXiv ID: `2611.20807v1`
- Authors: Wenrui Zhang, Yu Wang, Xiao Zhang
- Published: 2026-11-16
- Categories: `cs.DB`, `cs.AR`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20807v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.20807v1)

Emerging quantum applications like quantum communication generate large volumes of high-dimensional quantum data, requiring efficient data analysis and storage. However, classical database systems often struggle to meet requirements such as quantum processing or version management. This paper presents QuarkDB designed for efficient quantum data management. QuarkDB establishes a unified object model for relational and quantum data and defines the syntax of Quantum Structured Query Language. To support efficient quantum data management, QuarkDB integrates three key components: (1) a Hybrid Query Engine with cost-based hybrid plan selection over classical and quantum operators, (2) an Execution Engine with a three-level instructions intermediate representation, QuarkDB-IR, to support quantum execution and reduce quantum processing costs, and (3) a Storage Engine supporting quantum data version management, state reconstruction and data reuse. Compared with classical databases such as SQLite and PostgreSQL or quantum databases like Qute, our QuarkDB achieves query-latency speedups ranging from 2.14x to 25.86x speedup under extensive real-world workloads.

### 5. UniGraph: A Unified Graph-based Index for Dynamic Hybrid Dense-Sparse Vector Retrieval

- arXiv ID: `2611.20758v1`
- Authors: Keyu Chen, Xiaoyao Zhong, Jiabao Jin, Peng Cheng, Wangze Ni, Jingkuan Song, Heng Tao Shen
- Published: 2026-11-16
- Categories: `cs.IR`, `cs.DB`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20758v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.20758v1)

Hybrid dense-sparse vector retrieval combines semantic signals from dense representations with lexical signals from sparse representations, typically ranking items by a query-dependent weighted sum of dense and sparse vector distances. Existing systems commonly search separate dense and sparse vector indexes and rerank the union of their candidates, incurring two retrieval passes and potentially discarding items that rank highly only after the semantic and lexical signals are combined. Constructing a graph-based index directly under a hybrid distance can mitigate this premature candidate loss. However, because varying the hybrid weight alters the underlying neighborhood relationships, a single static graph built at a fixed weight cannot accommodate dynamic query-specific weights. Therefore, we propose a Unified Hybrid Graph (UniGraph), which merges neighbor sets computed at multiple hybrid weights into a single bounded-degree graph index, enabling hybrid vector retrieval with different query-specific weights without index reconstruction. We further develop an adaptive weight-guided search framework that chooses between sparse- and dense-guided entry points according to the query weight, together with a dense-first hybrid distance pruning strategy that avoids unnecessary exact sparse distance computations. Extensive experiments show that UniGraph provides a favorable Recall-QPS trade-off across different query weights. In the mixed-weight setting, UniGraph delivers 27.6%~52.3% higher QPS than the fastest baseline on each dataset at Recall@100 greater than or equal to 0.95. UniGraph has been deployed at scale in Ant Group's production environment, where hybrid retrieval with query-dependent weights is in strong demand.

### 6. Optimizing Query Decomposition for Reliable Validation

- arXiv ID: `2611.20511v1`
- Authors: Shuo Zhang, Sudeepa Roy, Jun Yang
- Published: 2026-11-16
- Categories: `cs.DB`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20511v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.20511v1)

Large language models (LLMs) have made querying databases more accessible, but cannot guarantee that the SQL they generate is correct. Without a trusted reference query, a validator (human or LLM) must decide whether a generated query answers the intended question. Enterprise questions often require queries with multiple joins, nested subqueries, and multi-level aggregation, and validating such a query in one judgment is error-prone. Decomposition simplifies this task by rewriting a query into smaller queries (blocks) that recompose to the original, each checked against its own sub-question.
  Decompositions differ, however: smaller blocks make each judgment easier but require more judgments, and the best balance depends on the validator's ability. We formulate validation-aware query decomposition as an optimization problem. Just as a query optimizer chooses among equivalent plans by estimated cost, we choose among decompositions by how reliably a given validator is expected to check them. We model how task difficulty and validator ability jointly affect reliability and define an objective that balances reliability against the number of judgments. Over a space of decompositions derived from logical-plan rewrites, our search finds an optimal one. We implement it in DecoVal, a pipeline that presents each block with its sub-question and input interface, combines local verdicts into a query-level decision, and localizes errors to blocks.
  A user study shows that decomposition helps human validators catch and locate errors: with DecoVal, participants caught 91% of the errors, up from 36% without decomposition, and located the error in nearly every rejection. Experiments on BEAVER and Spider 2.0 with LLM validators show that DecoVal also raises error-detection recall over whole-query validation while keeping precision high, and that the best granularity depends on the validator.

### 7. CapCover Filter: Semantic Approximate Membership Queries over Embeddings

- arXiv ID: `2611.20132v1`
- Authors: Dvir David Biton, Shay Hahiashvili, Nadav Keren, Gil Einziger, Roy Friedman
- Published: 2026-11-15
- Categories: `cs.DB`, `cs.AI`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20132v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.20132v1)

Determining whether an embedding is sufficiently similar to any vector in a vector store without performing a nearest-neighbor search can substantially improve query throughput in vector-store systems. We study Semantic Approximate Membership Queries (SAMQs), which allow queries to be rejected when no indexed vector meets the requested similarity threshold, while guaranteeing no false negatives.
  We introduce CapCover Filter, a geometric data structure that summarizes a set of embeddings using spherical caps. Its representation is independent of the query threshold, allowing a single filter to support arbitrary thresholds without reconstruction. We evaluate alternative construction and quantization methods, compare CapCover Filter with competing filters, and measure its impact on query throughput.
  Across five real-world datasets, at a cosine similarity threshold of 0.95, CapCover Filter eliminates approximately 20--100\% of index accesses while using only 2\% of the raw (FP32) embedding memory. When placed before a FAISS flat index, it improves query throughput by up to 382\% and by approximately 116\% on average across the five datasets, while returning the same range-search result. We also evaluate lower similarity thresholds. Our results show that CapCover Filter can substantially improve vector-store throughput when similarity thresholds are tight, retrieval is expensive, and indexed embeddings are highly clustered. We release an optimized implementation of CapCover Filter and its evaluation framework.

Point-of-interest (POI) databases support location-based services, yet urban changes quickly make their records stale and manual maintenance is costly. These results demonstrate the effectiveness and practical value of MAPS for scalable POI maintenance.

### 10. HyperDBDiag: Multi-Root-Cause Identification and Operator-Level Localization for Database Performance Anomalies

- arXiv ID: `2611.19241v1`
- Authors: Hu Wang, Zhengjin Yan, Hong Zhu, Qiong Zuo
- Published: 2026-11-14
- Categories: `cs.DB`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19241v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.19241v1)

Database performance anomalies in modern production environments are not always triggered by a single cause. Instead, they often arise from the concurrent interaction of multiple causes within the same time window, posing substantial challenges to accurate operator-level root-cause analysis. When multiple root causes interact through shared resources, metric anomalies may be amplified, attenuated, or masked. Consequently, salient local anomalies may no longer correspond directly to the actual root causes, making conventional single-path diagnosis and shallow correlation-based methods prone to misdiagnosis. To address this challenge, we propose HyperDBDiag, a method for multi-root-cause identification and operator-level localization of database performance anomalies. HyperDBDiag connects root causes, performance metrics, SQL templates, and execution operators in a knowledge graph, captures joint anomaly patterns across metrics with a metric hypergraph, and uses a constrained LLM to determine the final root-cause set and provide remediation suggestions. When finer-grained localization is possible, HyperDBDiag also identifies the associated anomalous SQL statements and execution operators. We evaluate HyperDBDiag on a database performance anomaly dataset constructed using DB-MAGS. Experimental results show that HyperDBDiag effectively reduces graph complexity while improving root-cause localization accuracy.

### 11. HorizANN: Elastically Scaling Vector Search with Ghost Edges

- arXiv ID: `2611.19077v1`
- Authors: Talika Gupta, Yunqi Li, Aditya Kunte, Claire Lin, Supawit Chockchowwat, Daniel Waddington, Hubertus Franke, Yongjoo Park
- Published: 2026-11-14
- Categories: `cs.DB`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19077v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.19077v1)

Approximate nearest neighbor search (ANNS) is fundamental to modern data-intensive AI applications such as retrieval-augmented generation, recommendation systems, and semantic search. Among existing indexing methods, graph-based indexes emerge as the state of the art due to their superior recall-throughput tradeoffs. Scaling these indexes to multiple machines requires partitioning the data and building indexes on each. However, when new machines are added or removed, the data must be repartitioned and the indexes reconstructed from scratch, making horizontal scaling costly at billion scale. In this work, we present HorizANN, a distributed graph-based ANNS system that scales horizontally without requiring full index reconstruction. To achieve this, we introduce ghost edges, a mechanism for preserving cross-partition connectivity as partition boundaries change during repartitioning. By reusing the structural information that would otherwise be discarded during repartitioning, HorizANN avoids costly full index reconstruction. As the index grows from 100M vectors on one worker to 1B vectors on ten workers, HorizANN inserts each 100M vectors within 2.5 hours and sustains 3.4--3.5K queries per second at 0.998 Recall@10.

### 12. DataTrace: Benchmarking Data Agents for Evidence-Grounded Root-Cause Localization

- arXiv ID: `2611.18926v1`
- Authors: Tung Sum Thomas Kwok, Xiaotian Lin, Yuyu Luo
- Published: 2026-11-13
- Categories: `cs.DB`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18926v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.18926v1)

Database agents diagnose pipeline failures, but a correct root cause is trustworthy only when it is supported by evidence the agent inspected. Existing benchmarks usually grade the final query or answer but fail to distinguish an evidence-based diagnosis from an unsupported correct answer. We introduce DataTrace, a benchmark for evidence-grounded root-cause localization over typed graphs of database artifacts. DataTrace uses controlled fault injection to derive root causes and evidence paths within 83 runnable environments without human annotations, and scores root-cause correctness and coverage of the supporting path separately. We also introduce CoSE, a generator-solver protocol in which an external verifier admits reproducible faults and checks both the submitted answer and the inspected evidence. Our evaluation shows that seven proprietary models achieve average root-cause accuracy 48%, with a mean per-model conditional unsupported rate of 18%. Fine-grained analysis shows that this gap varies sharply with fault mechanism, dependency distance, and evidence modality. After in-loop training on a matched held-out batch, the share of grounded correct diagnoses rises from 83.7% to 94.6%, while root-cause accuracy rises from 29.2% to 36.7%. These results show that while final-answer accuracy can hide unsupported diagnoses, evidence coverage provides an actionable signal to improve database agents. Code and data are available at https://hkustdial.github.io/datatrace/.

### 13. Agent-Native by Design: A Programming Language for Agentic Applications

- arXiv ID: `2611.21298v1`
- Authors: Jayanaka Dantanarayana, Savini Kashmira, Xiaoyu Ma,  Malitha, Malitha Prabhashana, Lingjia Tang, Jason Mars
- Published: 2026-11-16
- Categories: `cs.PL`, `cs.AI`, `cs.SE`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21298v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.21298v1)

Building agentic applications requires combining large language model (LLM) operations, workflow structure and application state, and model-guided routing. With SDKs and agent frameworks, developers connect these components through supporting code that prepares model inputs, propagates state, and dispatches model-selected steps. Workflow changes can require coordinated updates to this code, increasing development and testing effort. Splitting execution logic between application code and framework internals also limits compiler visibility into control flow and state dependencies, hindering automatic optimization. We introduce Agent-Native Programming, which unifies these three components with shared execution semantics. We realize this approach in Jac, a Python-superset language, through "by llm()" for typed model operations and tool use, Object-Spatial Programming for workflow structure and state, and "visit ... by llm()" for model-guided routing. The routing construct derives the model's choices from executable workflow steps and schedules the selected steps. This explicit connection between model decisions and program execution gives the compiler and runtime a common basis for dependency analysis and automatic optimization. Across five benchmarks and five baselines, Jac achieves a 1.8-8.7x reduction in AI-integration code relative to the OpenAI SDK, with competitive observed task quality and lower coding-agent development effort. Two case studies demonstrate speculative execution and service partitioning through configuration alone, using program analysis and runtime checks to preserve workflow behavior under stated conditions.

### 14. Noise-Aware Testing of NISQ Quantum Programs: Self-Baseline Oracle with a Controlled False-Positive Rate

- arXiv ID: `2611.20613v1`
- Authors: Mir Mohammad Yousuf, Shabir Ahmad Sofi
- Published: 2026-11-16
- Categories: `quant-ph`, `cs.SE`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20613v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.20613v1)

Testing quantum programs on Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) devices faces a test-oracle problem: hardware noise and finite-shot sampling make the runs of a correct program vary as much as those of a faulty one, so oracles with a fixed acceptance threshold produce false positives that depend on the program and the backend. We present a noise-aware testing framework built on self-baseline calibration: each run of the program under test is compared with repeated executions of a trusted reference program on the same backend, and the acceptance threshold is the $(1-α)$ quantile of the finite-shot null distribution of the Jensen-Shannon divergence, so the oracle's false-positive rate is a level set in advance. A noncentral chi-square model predicts detection power, a homogeneity test and a two-run sentinel detect drift, a mutation engine labels every mutant exactly within a three-class noise-aware taxonomy, and zero-noise extrapolation attributes rejections to faults or noise. In simulation on nine core benchmarks, 32 QASMBench programs, ten real bugs, seven noise regimes and three device snapshots, the oracle's false-positive rate is 0.0011 at $α= 10^{-3}$ over 9,000 correct runs, against 36-74% for fixed thresholds, 8.9% for a QOIN-style filter and 10.8% for a globally calibrated threshold, while attaining the highest F1 on non-equivalent faults (0.941). On an IBM Heron device, using about 14 minutes of QPU time, it rejected none of 82 correct runs and detected 120 of 121 faults and semantic mutants, and a two-run sentinel reduced drift-induced false positives from 27.4% to 1.8%.

### 15. A Testing Method for Detector Error Models in Quantum Error Correction Compilers

- arXiv ID: `2611.20501v1`
- Authors: Junjie Luo, Yasunari Suzuki
- Published: 2026-11-16
- Categories: `cs.SE`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20501v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.20501v1)

A quantum error correction (QEC) compiler produces, from a code and a noise model, both the physical circuit to execute and a detector error model (DEM). The DEM lists the probability of each error mechanism and the measurements it flips. Decoders consume the DEM without verification, so a defect that describes the noise incorrectly calibrates the decoder against wrong rates and silently degrades the logical error rate. Existing techniques for testing quantum software judge correctness by crashes or by statistics of sampled outputs. DEM defects that only distort rates lie below what sampling can resolve at the error rates where QEC operates. We present DEMVal, a testing method that validates each model the compiler emits against a reconstructed reference, with rates obtained from outside the compiler under test. It compares the two models exactly, so its sensitivity is independent of the physical error rate. The reference can be built in three ways that jointly cover the pipeline from source program to emitted model. Applied to NVIDIA's CUDA-Q QEC, Riverlane's Deltakit, and the deq component of Microsoft's QDK-EC, DEMVal found six defects, all confirmed by the developers and fixed. Sampling could have exposed only the four defects that drop, merge, or misplace mechanisms, and exposing all four requires millions of samples per experiment. Even analytically optimal moment tests miss the two rate-level defects, while exact model comparison detects all six with no samples. To our knowledge, DEMVal is the first systematic testing method for the error-model semantics of QEC compilers.

### 16. Learning from Mutants: Teaching Small Language Models to Synthesise Loop Invariants

- arXiv ID: `2611.19569v1`
- Authors: Daragh King, Vasileios Koutavas
- Published: 2026-11-14
- Categories: `cs.SE`, `cs.AI`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19569v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.19569v1)

Large language models (LLMs) have proven promising for synthesising loop invariants, a long-standing obstacle to automated program verification. Yet, frontier LLMs are typically accessible only through third-party APIs, hampering reproducibility and local deployment. Small language models (SLMs) sidestep these obstacles but require task-specific fine-tuning, for which high-quality training data is needed. We present a failure-driven data curation method for fine-tuning language models to synthesise loop invariants. Inspired by mutation testing, our approach applies semantic mutation operators to ground-truth invariants, producing semantic mutants that induce verification failure. Each mutant is paired with verifier feedback and its ground-truth counterpart, yielding training examples for feedback-guided invariant refinement. Whereas the vast space of mutants is a long-standing burden in mutation testing, it becomes an asset when generating training data, multiplying the examples obtainable from a fixed set of verified programs. We implement our approach for Dafny, augmenting an existing synthetic dataset of verified programs, and introduce a framework that iteratively refines candidate invariants using verifier feedback. On 472 DafnyBench programs, our best-performing fine-tuned Gemma-4-E4B configuration verifies 50.8% of programs zero-shot, compared to 8.1% for the base model, a 6.3$\times$ improvement. Higher-order mutants prove more effective as training data than first-order mutants, and adding mutation-based examples to direct synthesis examples, in which invariants are removed rather than mutated, improves performance under iterative refinement. Our approach also transfers to Qwen3.5-4B, whose zero-shot verification rate rises from 9.3% to 53.6%.

### 17. Beyond Clumsy Agentic Tool Calling: How Cueing and Spotting Make the Difference

- arXiv ID: `2611.19275v1`
- Authors: Xindi Yang, Yan Liu, Jiajun Sun
- Published: 2026-11-14
- Categories: `cs.AI`, `cs.SE`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19275v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.19275v1)

LLM-based task-oriented agents depend on tools to interact with their environments, but may overlook execution feedback, repeat ineffective calls, or persist with unproductive strategies. These behaviors can hinder agents' ability to use tools autonomously and sustain task progress. We characterize tool-use symptoms at two temporal granularities: local clumsy tool-use behaviors and tool-use detours spanning multiple interactions. Towards greater autonomy in tool use, we present ToolPulse, an online middleware supporting autonomous tool use through cueing and spotting. ToolPulse tracks execution state and temporal interaction patterns, monitoring proposed calls before dispatch and inspecting execution feedback prior to the agent resumes reasoning. It combines state-based rules, semantic sequence analysis, and interaction-feature similarity to identify intervention opportunities and provide verbal reminders, structured feedback, or context-specific advice from auxiliary models. Our process-oriented evaluation distinguishes trigger validity, behavioral cessation, and substantive correction alongside task outcomes and resource costs. Using SWE-agent with three backbone models, we evaluate 1,350 trajectories on 90 Python, C++, and Java bug-fix tasks, comparing unassisted execution with four assistance strategies. Substantive responses follow 61.6-90.9% of valid triggers for tool-use detours. Concise reminders achieve the highest observed aggregate substantive-response rate; more elaborate assistance offers symptom-dependent benefits and can be less effective. Aggregate repair outcomes remain close to baseline, with token savings in selected settings. These findings support proactive runtime assistance for autonomous tool use. The gap between behavioral adjustment and aggregate repair gains calls for careful decisions on intervention timing and assistance strategy in autonomous agents.

### 18. Live Noise Fingerprints for Quantum Program Analysis

- arXiv ID: `2611.19143v1`
- Authors: Avner Bensoussan, Karine Even-Mendoza, Debalina Banerjee, Elena Chachkarova, Sophie Fortz, Vasileios Klimis, Mohammad Reza Mousavi
- Published: 2026-11-14
- Categories: `cs.SE`, `quant-ph`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19143v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.19143v1)

Quantum programs are probabilistic in nature, and noisy execution due to current imperfect hardware further complicates their analysis. In this paper, we study abstract semantics for analysing quantum programs under these conditions. We introduce noise fingerprints, compact descriptions of the observable effects of noise relative to a finite interface of input states and output measurements. We use them to define our abstract domain with respect to the concrete noise-related behaviour. We relate the concrete and abstract domains through a probabilistic Galois connection, extending the Galois-connection framework to distribution-valued interpretations. We study the resulting abstraction in terms of observational soundness, conciseness, efficiency, and ability to adapt to changing noise. We show that polynomial-size interfaces yield exponentially smaller fingerprints than full noise models while preserving the chosen observations, and that fingerprint distance characterises the largest observational discrepancy over the chosen interface. Further, we develop ChanShadow, which reuses noise fingerprints of commonly used short program fragments to analyse noisy execution of whole programs and provides a uniform finite-sample error bound. We construct working examples showing how fingerprints support program analysis. We evaluate fingerprint precision, representation cost, measurement requirements, and behaviour under noise drift. On a synthetic benchmark, fingerprints retain $(99 \pm 1)\%$ of the noise strength information of a full noise model. On simulated noise drift, reusing earlier measurements reduces the number of fresh measurement rounds needed to reach an error tolerance of $0.10$ by a factor of $2.3$ at $95\%$ confidence and by a factor of $27$ under the median-error criterion.

- Categories: `cs.SE`, `cs.AI`

Source code is often obfuscated through identifier renaming, incentivizing reverse engineers to develop techniques for recovering meaningful names. To assess how LLMs compare with these techniques, we evaluate 4 traditional baselines and 3 LLMs (Qwen, OLMo and DeepSeek) on identifier detection and semantic recovery across 51 recent Java projects, 3 obfuscators, and 6 context/retrieval settings. LLMs generally outperform traditional techniques on both tasks, but benefit from different forms of context and settings: agentic retrieval yields the strongest detection results, reaching 91.56% $F_1$, while enclosing-class context generally improves semantic recovery at a higher token cost. Despite these gains, exact recovery peaks at 16.42% overall (under one-hop context) and 32.93% for field names (under repository retrieval), which are the easiest to recover. Class identifiers are the hardest to detect, with a mean detection $F_1$ of 48.48% across models, settings and obfuscators. More context is not always beneficial: larger methods hinder semantic recovery. Beyond identifier renaming, our experiments show that control-flow obfuscation degrades both tasks more than string encryption. Our findings provide useful ad hoc insights for reverse engineers choosing models and context strategies to deobfuscate code snippets.

### 20. From Language-Specific to Cross-DSL: Semantic-Role-Guided Model Completion with LLMs

- arXiv ID: `2611.18952v1`
- Authors: Meriem Ben Chaaben, Théo Giraudet, Benoit Combemale, Houari Sahraoui
- Published: 2026-11-13
- Categories: `cs.SE`, `cs.AI`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18952v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.18952v1)

The rapid emergence of large language models (LLMs), trained on vast and diverse corpora, has positioned them as a promising source of domain knowledge to assist modelers. However, leveraging LLMs for model completion remains challenging: suggestions must respect both the syntax of a domain-specific language (DSL) and the intended domain semantics, while remaining consistent with an evolving model. This paper presents RAFiC, an LLM-based approach for iterative domain model completion that generalizes across DSLs. It drives completion using metamodel information to ensure syntactic validity and enriches prompts with semantic-role annotations to better capture the intended modeling role of DSL elements. The approach provides context-aware, incremental guidance during model construction. We evaluate RAFiC through an ablation-based comparative study over three DSLs and two scenarios per DSL to isolate the contribution of syntactic and semantic guidance. Across these six scenarios, semantic role guidance improves contribution rate in every case, and RAFiC improves completion effectiveness over weaker prompting configurations in most of them. The largest gains occur for DSLs that are less represented in LLM training data. In these cases, iterative guidance leads to earlier and more stable accepted suggestions, while semantic role guidance improves their relevance.
