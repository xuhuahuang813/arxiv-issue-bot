### 1. AgentOnto: Ontology-Guided LLM Agents for Enterprise Data Analytics

- arXiv ID: `2610.23317v1`
- Authors: Dayan Pan, Bincheng Zuo, Xiaojian Wang, Daogang Zhang, Jingyuan Wang
- Published: 2026-10-16
- Categories: `cs.AI`, `cs.DB`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23317v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.23317v1)

Enterprise data analysis requires agents to connect analytical requests with knowledge distributed across databases, business documents, and existing example tasks. Without an explicit representation of this knowledge, an agent may produce incorrect analytical results and cannot reliably incorporate feedback. We present AgentOnto, an ontology-guided LLM agent for enterprise data analytics. AgentOnto organizes heterogeneous enterprise knowledge into an ontology that directly guides analysis and incorporates confirmed feedback. Iterative Ontology Preparation further improves the ontology through self-generated analysis tasks. We evaluate AgentOnto on two public data sources. AgentOnto substantially outperforms representative methods, while module-level evaluations confirm the effectiveness of ontology construction, analysis, and updating. A real-world deployment further demonstrates its practical value in enterprise analytics. These results demonstrate that explicitly constructing, using, and updating enterprise knowledge supports reliable and adaptable data analysis.

### 2. Designing for Imperfect Prompts: A Simulation-Based Evaluation of an LLM-Based Enterprise Data Assistant Using Knowledge Graphs

- arXiv ID: `2610.23304v1`
- Authors: Niklas Wagner, Alexander Mädche
- Published: 2026-10-16
- Categories: `cs.HC`, `cs.AI`, `cs.DB`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23304v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.23304v1)

Enterprise data assistants using large language models (LLMs) promise democratized data access in organizations. Yet in our real-world deployment within the CFO unit's data lake at an industry partner, we observed that business users frequently pose imperfect prompts that include domain-specific vocabulary and lack schema references or structural information. This Design Science Research study examines automatic resolution using a semantic mediation layer. We design and evaluate two design variants: schema-based mediation via semantic similarity, and knowledge graph-based mediation leveraging an automatically constructed knowledge graph from the data lake. Our simulation-based evaluation using real-world data, supported by LLM-as-a-Judge assessments and human validation, shows that knowledge graph-based mediation outperforms the schema-based approach in Precision, Recall, F1, and preserving user intent. We propose a design principle for knowledge graph-based mediation, demonstrating that structured knowledge representation bridges user intent and database structure and guides the design of enterprise data assistants across skill levels.

### 3. An Error Taxonomy for Property Graph Data

- arXiv ID: `2610.23083v1`
- Authors: Valerie Restat, Dominique Hausler, Désirée Bruns, Uta Störl
- Published: 2026-10-16
- Categories: `cs.DB`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23083v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.23083v1)

Graph data are increasingly used in applications where relationships and topology matter. Yet, compared to tabular data, the notions of data quality and error types for graphs are less established. Tabular error concepts often fail to capture structural properties and context inherent in graph data, hindering systematic quality assessment in graph-based systems. We focus on property graphs (PGs) in graph databases. PGs are often managed without a prescriptive schema, enabling data insertion without explicit validation, while still exhibiting an implicit schema of recurring structural patterns. Irregularities in these patterns may indicate errors, but their interpretation depends on the surrounding graph context. To address these challenges, we provide a systematic view of errors in graph data by proposing a comprehensive graph-specific taxonomy of error types. To position these errors with respect to established work on tabular data quality, we derive a mapping between graph errors and corresponding error notions in tabular data. This mapping highlights which concepts transfer across data models and where structural properties of graphs introduce fundamentally different challenges requiring extensions. Overall, the taxonomy and mapping provide a shared vocabulary for describing, characterizing, comparing, and studying graph data errors, and they lay the groundwork for future work on error detection and repair in graph data.

### 4. NL2SQL with Iterative Self-Refinement

- arXiv ID: `2610.22968v1`
- Authors: Anjali Vemula, Akshat Dasula
- Published: 2026-10-16
- Categories: `cs.DB`, `cs.AI`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22968v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.22968v1)

Bridging the gap between human intent and machine logic remains a critical challenge in data access for non-technical users, yet Large Language Models (LLMs) remain unreliable: they hallucinate schema elements, generate syntactically invalid queries, or fail to capture user intent. These limitations are especially critical in domains such as insurance, where KPIs, regulatory constraints, and legacy relational systems demand high precision. We introduce a multi-agent, persona-driven NL2SQL framework that combines (1) domain relevance detection, (2) schema-grounded knowledge construction, (3) KPI-aware SQL synthesis, (4) syntax-level self-repair using sqlite execution feedback, and (5) semantic consistency checking via SQL-to-NL reverse engineering. Our system significantly reduces query hallucination and improves semantic fidelity compared to direct LLM prompting. Evaluated on 60 queries (50 from a HuggingFace text-to-SQL dataset and 10 custom domain queries), the system achieves 90% correctness using Mistral across seven coordinated LLM calls showing that this approach can substantially increase the reliability of NL2SQL pipelines in high-stakes environments.

### 5. FuseRAG: Fusing Structure and Evidence into Inference for Cost-Efficient Graph-based RAG

- arXiv ID: `2610.22818v1`
- Authors: Tingqi Wang, Yingshu Li, Zhipeng Cai
- Published: 2026-10-16
- Categories: `cs.DB`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22818v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.22818v1)

Graph-based retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) exploits relationships among evidence to support multi-hop question answering. However, conveying retrieved graphs through non-unique linear textual contexts to Large Language Models (LLMs) leads to graph-structure degradation and inefficient KV-cache reuse. Meanwhile, graph-structure order optimization is already NP-hard, and the additional KV-cache reuse objective further complicates the search by introducing potentially conflicting orders. To tackle these issues, we propose FuseRAG, a cost-efficient graph-based RAG framework that shifts most retrieved evidence from explicit textual contexts to latent representations. Our main idea is to separate reusable evidence content from query-aware structural interactions in latent space, while retaining only a few explicit facts as semantic anchors. The resulting latent representations are injected into decoder self-attention, where they reshape attention over evidence and thereby influence hidden states and next-token probabilities. This design preserves graph structure through latent interactions, enables evidence reuse beyond exact token-prefix matching. Experiments on three multi-hop question-answering datasets with Llama-3-8B and Qwen3.5-9B show that FuseRAG improves answer quality while achieving up to 2.7x and 2.2x TTFT speedups over the fastest graph-based baseline, respectively.

### 6. The Case for TPU-Accelerated Analytical Query Processing

- arXiv ID: `2610.22673v1`
- Authors: Yeounoh Chung, Jaehyun Ha, Helena Caminal, Fatma Ozcan, Aditya K Kamath, Arvind Krishnamurthy, Henry M. Levy
- Published: 2026-10-15
- Categories: `cs.DB`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22673v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.22673v1)

Large-scale analytical query processing requires computing substrates capable of immense distributed scale-out. Tensor Processing Units (TPUs) perfectly embody this extreme-scale architecture, offering massive computational density and highly scalable optical pod-level interconnects. Despite these advantages, TPUs have so far remained largely unexplored for analytical database workloads. In this paper, we present the first comprehensive case for TPU-accelerated analytical query processing by running all the TPC-H queries entirely on the TPUs. We introduce Native TPU Accelerator (NTA) for analytical query processing, which provides hardware-conscious algorithm design and optimizations in JAX/XLA. By exposing the TPU's structural mechanics, we demonstrate how dynamic, memory-bound database operations can be rewritten into deterministic JAX programs. We engineer a suite of novel execution strategies that leverage both the TPU's TensorCore and SparseCore to trade sparse, unpredictable memory accesses for dense, hardware-aligned ones. At the core of NTA, a compiler uses catalog statistics to assign guarded static capacities and a hardware-calibrated local cost model to select a TPU-native implementation for each relational operator before JIT compilation.
  We evaluate our system using the TPC-H benchmark. Taking full advantage of TPU slices and JAX distributed computing, we seamlessly scale our execution environment to run TPC-H benchmark up to Scale Factor 1000 (SF1000). Evaluated against a rigorous CPU ground truth (DuckDB), our native TPU accelerator achieves state-of-the-art performance, achieving a 7.0x single-device speedup over DuckDB at SF100 and a 68.6x speedup with 16 TPUs at SF1000 (1TB). The results establish TPUs not merely as dense neural compute hardware, but as a highly competitive, massively scalable substrate for the next generation of analytical query processing.

### 7. Efficient Dialect-Agnostic SQL Equivalence Refutation

- arXiv ID: `2610.22442v1`
- Authors: Junwen An, Manuel Rigger
- Published: 2026-10-15
- Categories: `cs.DB`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22442v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.22442v1)

Identifying semantically inequivalent SQL queries is useful for many applications, such as evaluating Text-to-SQL and dialect translation systems. However, existing approaches often struggle with the diversity of SQL features and dialects, requiring significant manual effort and expertise to extend query feature support across Database Management Systems (DBMSs). To address these limitations, we present SQLWitness, an efficient, DBMS-agnostic query equivalence refutation engine. Our core idea is leveraging the DBMS execution engine to determine whether generated instances constitute counterexamples, thereby faithfully capturing the semantics of the SQL dialect and preventing false counterexamples. To efficiently generate test data, our approach explores a constrained data-generation space and uses an integrity-constraint-aware generation strategy. Furthermore, to guide time-efficient termination of this test-data generation process, we pair predicate-coverage signals with a statistical estimator. We extensively evaluated SQLWitness on six benchmark suites across four SQL dialects. SQLWitness refuted 5.34% to 88.4% more pairs than state-of-the-art baselines, and processed query pairs in 0.12 seconds on average, achieving a speedup from 2.1x to 16.4x over the baselines. Furthermore, in a case study, SQLWitness found 8.4% inconsistent translations within a dialect translation benchmark. We believe SQLWitness could be broadly adopted for many use cases.

### 8. An Interactive GraphRAG Workspace for Reifying, Auditing, and Steering Conversational AI Memories

- arXiv ID: `2610.23559v1`
- Authors: Munshi Md Arafat Hussain, Md. Abdur Rahman, Md Saeed Siddik
- Published: 2026-10-16
- Categories: `cs.SE`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23559v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.23559v1)

Conversational Large Language Models (LLMs) are widely employed in software engineering for code generation, debugging, and architectural design. However, as developers engage in multi-session development tasks, technical knowledge becomes siloed in isolated, linear chat histories. Reusing context across sessions leads to three critical challenges: (1) flat-vector retrieval fails to traverse multi-hop relational dependencies between distant architectural decisions; (2) temporal drift in software packages and APIs results in silent context poisoning when reusing aged dialogue logs; and (3) raw conversation concatenation induces prompt bloat and attention degradation.
  We present CognitiveCanvas, an interactive tool that transforms conversational AI memories from ephemeral text logs into persistent, manipulable, and audited knowledge objects. \textsc{CognitiveCanvas} integrates: (1) a Hierarchical Semantic Fact-Graph (HSFG) that distills dialogues into Goal, Fact, and Code artifacts, achieving a 92.2\% token reduction while enabling multi-hop GraphRAG traversal; (2) a Continuous Temporal Drift Auditor coupling 60+ regex heuristics with live calendar-anchored LLM verification for 1-click self-correction; and (3) a Spatial-Symbolic Prompt Compiler (SSPC) that translates 2D direct manipulation gestures (Voronoi clustering, node resizing, synthesis edges) directly into structured attention directives

### 9. Robust Function Calling for LLM Agents Under Interface Drift

- arXiv ID: `2610.23342v1`
- Authors: Aniruddha Chattopadhyay, Kanishka Halder, Partha Sarathi Roy
- Published: 2026-10-16
- Categories: `cs.AI`, `cs.CL`, `cs.SE`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23342v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.23342v1)

Tool-augmented language-model agents rely on interface text: tool descriptions, argument schemas, and the candidate tool list shown at inference time. These interfaces often change after deployment even when the underlying tool semantics remain fixed, creating a practical reliability problem for function-calling systems. We study this failure mode as interface drift and evaluate whether a lightweight, training-free repair layer can recover robustness without retraining the base model. Our method, SchemaShield-Lite, combines schema validation, candidate-set retry for missing tool names, and one-shot constrained repair. On Qwen3.5-9B, it raises DICE-300 accuracy from 0.717 to 0.813 and BFCL-200 accuracy from 0.580 to 0.805, recovering 19/22 and 33/45 drift-induced failures with no observed repair harms. Naive retry does not explain the gains. Ablations show that candidate-set drift, especially distractor tools, accounts for most observed damage, while description and schema edits alone are less harmful. The results position validation-and-repair as a practical robustness layer for deployed LLM-agent tool use.

### 10. VulnSAGE: Adjudicating SAST Reports by Connecting LLM Reasoning to Program Structure

- arXiv ID: `2610.23035v1`
- Authors: Jinbao Chen, Zixiang Zhou, Yu Zhang, Yihong Wu
- Published: 2026-10-16
- Categories: `cs.SE`, `cs.CR`, `cs.PL`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23035v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.23035v1)

Static Application Security Testing (SAST) reports flag operations whose safety depends on project-specific context, and maintainers must decide which reports describe real defects. Large language models (LLMs) can interpret this context, but adjudicating a report with them requires the decisive code, which often lies far from the reported line, and a decision basis that program analysis can check. We present VulnSAGE, an adjudication system for SAST reports that connects LLM reasoning to program structure in both directions. Its report-centered slicer recovers the dependencies of the reported operation together with the program structure that explains them, such as the caller object on which a helper acts. CLAIM, an intermediate representation for report judgments, records each model interpretation as a semantic fact that cites the program relations it assumes. Program analysis checks these premises, and the verdict follows from the facts whose premises hold. Both mechanisms share a program graph, which lets the model cite the code it reads and lets the checker query those citations. Statically supported true positives then guide proof-of-concept generation and runtime reproduction. Across 187 labeled reports from four corpora covering five vulnerability families, VulnSAGE rejects 89 of 97 false alarms and retains 85 of 90 real defects. Under a common PoC procedure, 25 of its positive verdicts reproduce at runtime, compared with three for the LLM-based adjudicator LLM4SA. Applied to 45,440 alerts raised by five SAST tools on the 200 most-starred C/C++ projects on GitHub, it reproduces 104 defects at runtime. Maintainers have confirmed 24 of the 26 defects they have responded to, including ten previously unknown defects, nine of which are already fixed.

### 11. No One-Size-Fits-All: Adaptive Code Editing with Feature-Based Strategy Selection

- arXiv ID: `2610.22881v1`
- Authors: Jun Wan, Zhongxin Liu, Dajun Chen, Wei Jiang, Yong Li, Xiaoxue Ren
- Published: 2026-10-16
- Categories: `cs.SE`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22881v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.22881v1)

Large language models have shown strong potential in automating code editing (CE) tasks, yet most existing systems rely on a fixed CE strategy (i.e., one-step, search-replace, and plan-apply) across diverse scenarios. In practice, CE tasks vary widely in code context, edit locality, and semantic complexity, causing no single strategy to perform consistently well. Moreover, systematic studies on the relationship between strategies and CE tasks remain scarce, limiting further optimization of LLM-based code editing strategies. To address this gap, we construct CEBench, comprising 500 real-world CE tasks curated from SWE-bench. Then we compare three representative CE strategies (one-step, search-replace, plan-apply). Our study reveals two key findings: (1) each strategy exhibits distinct advantages for specific CE tasks, with no universal optimality; (2) strategy effectiveness is significantly influenced by four core task features: total lines of code, modification type, modification scope, and task type. Motivated by these findings, we propose Ace, an adaptive code editing framework that dynamically selects optimal CE strategies based on task features. Unlike classifier-based or instruction-prompting approaches, Ace employs a fine-tuned LLM that learns feature-strategy mappings from empirical data to recommend strategies with interpretable rationales. Extensive experiments on our CEBench-verified show that Ace outperforms state-of-the-art baselines: it boosts Pass@1 by 21.7% while reducing token consumption by 27.3% compared to the best baseline (AutoCodeRover). Our strategy selector achieves 72.6% selection accuracy with 69.9% rationale accuracy (Cohen's Kappa = 0.807), providing transparent and trustworthy decision-making for developers.

### 12. ForTress: Bringing Assurance and Determinism for LLM-Driven Fortran-to-Python Translation

- arXiv ID: `2610.22528v1`
- Authors: Qinrun Dai, Ruitong Liu, Chien-Wei Huang, Guanhong Tao, Guannan Wei, Yueqi Chen
- Published: 2026-10-15
- Categories: `cs.SE`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22528v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.22528v1)

Modernizing mature scientific software from Fortran to Python can improve accessibility and integration with modern computing tools, but manual rewriting is expensive. Large language models (LLMs) make translation more practical, yet generated code can pass regression tests while violating source-language semantics, and contextual corrections may not persist across subsequent translations. We present ForTress, which solidifies recurring LLM translation decisions into guarded executable rules for deterministic reuse and provides semantic assurance for a non-trivial subset of these rules. Its LLM-assisted auditing process expresses universally quantified preservation obligations over mechanized Fortran and Python semantics in Lean. Human experts review semantic fidelity and correspondence with production rules, and Lean's kernel checks proofs and refutations under explicit assumptions. These audit records expose the conditions either supporting correctness or identifying failure cases. We evaluate ForTress on 17 benchmarks from 15 scientific software spanning diverse domains. The translated programs achieve bitwise-identical with native Fortran on all evaluated tests under the recorded execution and initialization configurations. Against these software, ForTress accumulates 193 executable rules and 188 audit records, of which 121 have checked soundness proofs, including 31 established after counterexample-guided refinement. Cross-software reuse exceeds 90% for 14 of the 15 software. Even with additional assurance and determinism, the LLM usage cost is 44.2% lower than direct LLM translation.

### 13. AuditKIT: A Unified Evaluation Spine for Benchmarking, Judging, and Comparing Large Language Models and Agentic Systems

- arXiv ID: `2610.22511v1`
- Authors: Shreeyans Arora, Utsav Avaiya, Zera Lyngkhoi, Hem Gosalia, Ram Mohan Rao Kadiyala, Vinay Kumar Sankarapu, Pratinav Seth
- Published: 2026-10-15
- Categories: `cs.CL`, `cs.AI`, `cs.IR`, `cs.SE`
- Links: [abs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22511v1) | [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.22511v1)

Evaluating language models and agents requires combining benchmark accuracy, judge-based assessment, runtime performance, tool-use behavior, and retrieval quality. Differences in interfaces, metric semantics, and trace formats complicate these comparisons and can hide failures in agentic workflows. We present AuditKIT, a source-available evaluation library that records these measurements in one result representation, with direction-aware metric contracts, configuration fingerprints, and extensible metrics and backends. For agents, it provides turn-aware tool-call matching, separates parallel from sequential execution, and distinguishes verified task outcomes from trajectory-level diagnostics. Missing or unobservable evidence is marked unscored rather than counted as failure. For retrieval-augmented generation, it combines ranked retrieval metrics, judged grounding, and deterministic evidence checks. Seven case studies spanning model comparison, serving runtimes, retrieval, tool calling, and interoperability show how answer extraction, runtime behavior, and metric selection change evaluation outcomes.
